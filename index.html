What is Artificial Intelligence?

Artificial intelligence is computers working together to perform tasks associated with human intelligence, including recognizing objects, understanding language, finding patterns, solving problems, and making recommendations. All AI systems have been trained in ome manner using training or model data, from which then the machine identifies statistical patterns from those examples and then implements them to classify information or produce likely outputs (OECD.AI, n.d.).

Most AI today is narrow: it handles specific tasks rather than general human intelligence. Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI) corresponds to the first, most basic level of AI. Building on ANI, there is Artificial Generative Intelligence (AGI), and finally Artificial Superintelligence (ASI). At this moment, ASI is strictly theoretical as it describes a surpassing of combined human intelligence, creativity, strategic thinking, and decision-making, in any application you can think of.

The Evolution of AI

1950s: Beginning

Alan Turing proposed “thinking machines” and the Turing Test. John McCarthy coined the term artificial intelligence at the 1956 Dartmouth Conference.

1960s–1970s: Early programs

Programs such as ELIZA, an early chatbot, and Shakey the Robot demonstrated basic AI capabilities. Reduced progress and funding led to the first “AI winter.”

1980s–1990s: Slow progress

Researchers developed an early self-driving car in 1986. In 1997, IBM’s Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov.

2000–2019: AI growth

Faster computers, more data, and improved neural networks accelerated AI development. IBM Watson won Jeopardy! in 2011, and virtual assistants became widely available.

2020–present: AI surge

Large language models and generative systems—including GPT-3, DALL-E, and ChatGPT—greatly expanded AI’s ability to produce text and images and support practical applications.

What are opportunities and risks does AI present?

Opportunities

  1. Increased work efficiency
  2. Automation of repetitive tasks
  3. Translation services in real time
  4. Security (ex. flagging unusual transactions)
  5. 24/7 Tutoring and learning support (ex. explaining concepts, generating practice questions)
  6. Accessibility support (ex. text-to-speech, speech-to-text)
  7. Assistance in research, writing, and coding (ex. summarizing, comparing arguments)
  8. Prediction and planning (ex. energy system planning, weather forecasting)
  9. Medical diagnosis and drug discovery
  10. Pattern recognition in large datasets

AI has already solved mathematical problems humans thought to be unsolvable. It's pattern recognition capabilities are unmatched, well beyond what human intellect can ever achieve, which is leading to revolutionary new insights and discoveries.

Risks

  1. Bias and stereotypes in training data
  2. Privacy concerns
  3. Copyrighted work in training data
  4. Hallucinations: Generated answers that are wrong but sound certain (Amazon Staff, 2026)
  5. Deepfakes
  6. Autonomous agents that can act, not merely advise
  7. Operational and security risks, due to missing safeguards and continuous monitoring (OpenAI, 2026; Reuters, 2026)
  8. Service concentration and dependency on a few providers
  9. Increased energy consumption and environmental impact
  10. Existential risks from self-improving AI (theoretical)

The risks are real, but they can likely be mitigated with proper supervision, verification, and ethical considerations.

How should this information be interpreted?

The lesson is not to reject automation, but to match supervision to consequence. Payments, medical decisions, security changes, assessment, and legal advice need verified sources, access limits, audit logs, independent review, and a human being who monitors and controls the AI system. The NIST framework recommends managing AI risks throughout design, deployment, use, and evaluation rather than relying on a single safety test (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

What are some predictions and implications about AI?

In the future, AI could create major opportunities in healthcare, education, scientific research, accessibility, and environmental planning. More capable systems may help discover medicines, provide personalized tutoring, detect fraud, and manage energy more efficiently. However, increasing dependence on AI could also create serious risks. Criminals may use AI to produce deepfakes, automate cyberattacks, or identify security weaknesses. Organizations may become vulnerable to service outages if they depend on only a few cloud and AI providers. AI data centres also require large amounts of electricity. If electricity demand grows faster than power-grid capacity, some regions could experience emergency restrictions or an increased risk of blackouts. For these reasons, AI should support rather than completely replace human decision-making. High-risk actions should require human approval, reliable monitoring, verified information, and manual backup procedures. As AI becomes more capable, effective human supervision will become more important, not less.
